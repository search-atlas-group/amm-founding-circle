## Locked decisions and principles from the transcript

- Work that matters belongs in a lane; a lane is a meaningful outcome/project, not a task dump. (segments 265–268, 667–670)
- Keep the active board to roughly three or four lanes, plan no more than three weeks out, and make week one the densest. (321–324, 747–750)
- Every lane has exactly one owner; support is allowed, shared accountability is not. (352–362)
- The commander should not own every lane. The commander provides the final 10%: prioritization, context, correction, and support. (336–340, 527–529, 663–665)
- Cards are short, status-colored, and move to a done shelf when complete; the board is not a diary, task dump, or artifact wall. (276–317)
- Run a daily lane walk / standup, not a weekly drift cycle; keep meetings to six people or fewer, with four as the preferred size. (672–676, 753–761)
- Mission preparation comes before execution: define what good looks like, the scope, the map, and the handoff. (152–175)
- Automation is staged: prove the process with a dry run and human review before publishing, writing, or broad rollout. (715–729)
- Use AI for broad autonomous execution, but do not babysit it; attention is scarcer than tokens. (19–29, 169–175)

## Reusable teaching points

### Commander operating loop

`Brain dump → choose the one thing → draw 3–4 lanes → assign one owner → define week 1 → run the lane walk → refine/prioritize → move completed cards to done → repeat.`

### Lane quality test

A stranger should be able to look at the board and answer: What matters most? Who owns it? What is happening this week? What is blocked? What is complete? If not, the lane is too vague, too crowded, or being used as a log.

### Agent delegation test

Before starting an agent session, write the mission, source context, definition of done, constraints, expected artifacts, and review gate. Start it, then move on. Re-open only for a meaningful review or decision.

## Requests and follow-ups by workstream

### Agency operating system

- Build and use lanes for the agency’s highest-leverage outcomes.
- Separate fulfillment, command center, sales, and lead-pipeline concerns when the teams differ.
- Identify owners without assuming the founder must remain owner.
- Create a daily lane walk and a weekly/three-week horizon; do not overfill weeks two and three.

### Command center

- Fulfillment context: active services per client and real-time service performance.
- Revenue context: last/next billing date, amount, payment channel, payment status.
- Meeting context: meeting dates, next meeting, transcript repository, disposition, sentiment, and missed-meeting detection.
- Candidate sources named in the session: CRM/GHL, FreshBooks, Zoom API/webhook, GA4 and service dashboards. These are requirements to validate, not claims that access or connectors already exist. (569–586)

### Sales

- Audit four stages: traffic → CRM lead, pre-meeting/show rate, meeting experience/close, post-meeting follow-up. (603–610)
- Use actual system data to score the stage with greatest revenue leverage; compare data-driven ranking to operator judgment. (616–620)
- Map the customer journey, audit the current flow, document gaps, then define the offer/guarantee/offer ladder separately from the audit. (484–506)

### Fulfillment / SEO

- Classify skills into service buckets in the team repository.
- Dry-run CRO, technical SEO, content planning/writing, topical mapping, authority/link building, PR, and GBP-related work on a test client.
- Produce a mission plan first; do not publish press releases or other external work until the owner reviews and explicitly triggers execution. (715–729)
